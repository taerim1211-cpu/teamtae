British Cycling's "aggregation of marginal gains" is one of the most misquoted ideas in sports. The
usual retelling: improve everything by 1% and the improvements compound into something huge. True,
but it skips the part that actually mattered — someone had to decide *which* 1%s were worth chasing.

That's the harder problem. Most training plans fail not because people won't do the work, but
because they're optimizing the wrong variable. A recreational runner obsessing over shoe foam
compound while skipping sleep is doing 1% work on a 0.1% lever.

## Find the binding constraint first

Before adding anything — a new drill, a supplement, a recovery gadget — ask what's actually capping
performance right now. For most people outside elite competition, it's one of three things:

- **Consistency.** The plan you follow 80% of the time beats the perfect plan you follow 40% of
  the time.
- **Sleep.** It's the single highest-leverage recovery input and the one most easily sacrificed.
- **Technique under fatigue.** Form that holds up in the first ten minutes and falls apart after
  is where most injuries and performance ceilings come from.

Marginal gains only compound once the constraint that would otherwise erase them is handled. Chase
the 1%s after that, not instead of it.

## A simple test

If you can't say, in one sentence, what specific limiter a change addresses, it's probably not
worth adding yet. That filter alone removes most of the noise in a typical training plan.
