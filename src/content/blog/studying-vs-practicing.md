Rereading a chapter feels productive. Your eyes move across familiar sentences, the ideas nod along
in recognition, and the whole experience registers as "I know this." That feeling is one of the
least reliable signals in learning, and it's exactly why rereading is one of the least effective
study methods, despite being the most common one.

## Recognition is not recall

The problem is that recognizing information and retrieving it are different cognitive operations.
Seeing a fact and confirming "yes, that's familiar" uses a much shallower memory trace than
producing that fact from nothing. Exams, conversations, and real-world problems almost always
demand the second kind. Studying that only trains the first kind builds a skill you won't need.

This is why closing the book and trying to explain a concept out loud — badly, incompletely, from
memory — teaches you more in five minutes than another twenty minutes of highlighting.

## What to do instead

A few swaps, all well-supported by the cognitive science on this:

1. **Self-testing over rereading.** Close the material and try to reproduce it before checking.
2. **Spacing over massing.** Spread review across days instead of one long session; the forgetting
   in between is what makes the next retrieval attempt actually strengthen the memory.
3. **Interleaving over blocking.** Mixing related-but-distinct problem types trains you to
   recognize *which* method applies — usually the actual hard part.

None of this feels as smooth as rereading. That discomfort is the mechanism, not a side effect of
it.
