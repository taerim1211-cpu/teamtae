Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* is one of those books more people have opinions about
than have read. The "System 1 / System 2" framing gets cited constantly, usually flattened into
"intuition bad, deliberation good." That's not really the book's argument, and it misses the parts
that are actually useful.

## It's not that System 2 is smarter

System 2 — the slow, effortful, deliberate mode — is lazy and easily depleted. It frequently just
signs off on whatever System 1 already concluded, dressing up an intuitive judgment as a reasoned
one after the fact. The realistic goal isn't "use System 2 more." It's narrower: know which
specific situations reliably fool System 1, and build checks for those, because willpower alone
doesn't scale.

## The idea that's aged best: what you see is all there is

Kahneman's WYSIATI (What You See Is All There Is) is the quieter idea in the book, and probably the
most broadly useful one. Judgment doesn't fail because we reason badly from the evidence in front
of us — it fails because we don't notice how much evidence *isn't* in front of us, and we build
confident conclusions on an incomplete picture without registering the gap.

This explains a lot that "cognitive bias" as a label doesn't: confident first impressions from thin
information, the ease of constructing a coherent story from a few data points, and why adding more
information sometimes *decreases* confidence rather than increasing it — because it's the first
time the gaps become visible.

## One practical takeaway

Before trusting a confident conclusion — yours or someone else's — ask what information it would
need to change, and whether that information was even available to consider. Most bad judgments
aren't bad reasoning from the evidence. They're good reasoning from incomplete evidence, mistaken
for complete evidence.
