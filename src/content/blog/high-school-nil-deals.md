The headline version of high school NIL is simple: most states now let high school athletes earn money from their name, image, and likeness. That's true, but it's the least useful thing to know. The rules that actually shape what a deal can look like are the ones that don't make the headline, and they're surprisingly consistent from state to state.

## "Most states" still isn't every state

A handful of states, including Texas, Alabama, Michigan, and Indiana, still generally prohibit or sharply restrict deals tied to an athlete's athletic reputation. So the first step isn't finding a sponsor, it's checking your own state high school athletic association's policy directly. Not a summary, not a map someone posted, the association's own language. A deal that's fine one state over can cost eligibility at home.

## The one line every state draws

Wherever high school NIL is allowed, the same boundary shows up: it can't be a recruiting inducement. Money can't be the thing that gets an athlete to enroll at, or transfer to, a particular school. That's the rule that matters most, because it's the one that turns an ordinary endorsement into an eligibility problem.

It also explains who's kept out. Michigan's association allows individual endorsements but explicitly bars the school itself, coaches, boosters, and NIL collectives from arranging deals. Wisconsin's association put it more bluntly, saying collectives have no place in high school sports. The logic is the same in both: the people with a stake in where an athlete plays are exactly the people who can't be paying them.

## The financial aid timing nobody plans for

This is the part that catches families off guard, and it depends on which aid form a college uses:

- **FAFSA schools** use prior-prior-year tax data, so NIL income typically doesn't show up in aid calculations until roughly two years later.
- **CSS Profile schools** ask for projected earnings, so NIL income can reduce institutional aid in the same year it's earned.

Same deal, same dollars, very different effect depending on the college list. A family applying mostly to CSS Profile schools should treat NIL income as something that may come straight out of the aid package.

## What it looks like after high school

At the college level, the NIL Go clearinghouse run by Deloitte, launched in mid-2025, has cleared more than $355 million in deals through mid-2026 while rejecting tens of millions more that failed market-value checks. That's the direction things are heading: deals get scrutinized for whether they're real compensation or a disguised inducement. Building clean habits in high school, with real deals, real paperwork, and no one connected to the recruiting process involved, is good practice for that.

[CollegeIntel's guide to high school NIL deals](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/articles/high-school-nil-deals-explained) covers the state rules and aid mechanics in more depth. The short version: verify your state association's policy yourself, keep records like the self-employment income it is, and know which aid forms your target schools use before you sign anything.
