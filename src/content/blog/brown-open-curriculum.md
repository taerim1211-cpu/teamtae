Brown's Open Curriculum gets described, often by Brown itself, as freedom from general education requirements. Adopted in 1969 after student activism and a 400-plus page proposal called the Magaziner-Maxwell Report, it eliminated the standard distribution requirements most universities run on: no mandated courses across disciplines, no fixed core. The pitch was that students, not a checklist, should drive their own intellectual development.

## "Open" still means 30 courses and a concentration

The part that gets lost in the pitch: Open Curriculum isn't the absence of requirements, it's a different, much smaller set of them. Students still need at least 30 courses across eight semesters, still need to complete a concentration (Brown's word for a major, and there are roughly 80 of them), and still need to demonstrate writing competence. That's real structure. What's missing isn't structure, it's a distribution requirement telling you which eight departments those 30 courses have to touch.

## The actual mechanism is grading, not the absence of requirements

The feature that makes the curriculum work in practice isn't the lack of a core, it's Satisfactory/No Credit grading. Students can take most courses S/NC instead of for a letter grade, decided at registration and adjustable for the first four weeks, and exceptional S/NC work can even earn "S with Distinction" toward honors. That's what actually makes it safe to walk into a subject you have no background in. Without that, "no requirements" would just mean "figure out your own gen-ed while competing on GPA in classes you're unprepared for." The freedom is downstream of the grading policy, not the requirements policy.

## The part prospective students should actually worry about

The honest tension in a self-directed curriculum is whether an 18-year-old, admitted on a single short essay, can navigate a full four years without a fixed map. Brown's answer has historically been the Critical Review, a student-run course evaluation platform that helps people make that call informedly. The problem: participation in it has dropped roughly 27% between fall 2021 and fall 2024. A system that assumes good peer information gets meaningfully worse when the peer information dries up, and that's a real, current risk for anyone evaluating this specific school right now, not a hypothetical one.

If you're actually weighing this for yourself, [CollegeIntel's explainer on the Open Curriculum](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/articles/brown-open-curriculum-explained) goes deeper into the mechanics and history. But the one-line version: be honest about whether you do better with external structure or without it, lean on advisors and upperclassmen rather than assuming the course-review system will do the work for you, and don't assume any other school, Ivy or not, works this way. Brown's approach is specifically Brown's. Nobody else runs it like this.
