Winning more scholarship money than your bill seems to require sounds like the best kind of problem. In practice, "leftover" scholarship money covers three separate questions: whether the extra money is real, what the school does with it, and what the IRS thinks of it. Each one can shrink the surplus before you spend it.

## First, check whether the surplus is real

When outside scholarships push your total aid above the school's Cost of Attendance, many colleges don't hand you the difference. They practice **scholarship displacement**, which means cutting your need-based institutional grant first. Roughly half of students who win an outside scholarship see some reduction in their other aid.

A handful of states now limit this practice: Maryland (2017), New Jersey (2021), Pennsylvania, Washington, and California (all 2022), and Minnesota (2024). Everywhere else, ask the financial aid office directly how it treats outside awards before you assume the money is extra.

## How refunds work

If there really is a surplus, the school applies your aid to tuition, fees, and on-campus room and board. Then it generally refunds whatever is left directly to you. That refund is meant for real education-related costs like books, supplies, off-campus rent, groceries, and transportation.

## The tax part most students miss

Scholarships are tax-free only when they pay for **qualified education expenses**: tuition, required fees, and required course materials. Money that covers room and board, transportation, health fees, or other living costs counts as taxable income.

Here's a simple example. A $15,000 scholarship that covers $8,000 of tuition and $7,000 of room and board leaves you with $7,000 of taxable income, even though none of it ever looked like a paycheck. Keep your Form 1098-T and your receipts so you can show how the money was split.

There's also a 529 angle. If a student receives a tax-free scholarship, the 529 account owner can withdraw an equal amount without the usual 10% penalty. The withdrawal is still not tax-free, though. The earnings portion is taxed as income, and only the original contributions come out untaxed.

## Don't let it sit in checking

This is the trap that catches the most careful students. A refund left in a personal bank account counts as a reportable asset on next year's FAFSA. Student-owned assets are assessed at nearly four times the rate of parent-owned assets, so a balance that looks harmless can reduce next year's aid.

## What to do with it

- Ask the financial aid office whether it practices scholarship displacement, and get the answer in writing if you can.
- Track which dollars went to qualified expenses and which didn't, so tax time isn't a guessing game.
- Spend the refund on what it's for, pay down student loans, or move it into a 529 instead of letting it sit in checking before you file the FAFSA.

[CollegeIntel's guide to leftover scholarship money](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/articles/what-to-do-with-leftover-scholarship-money) goes through each scenario in more detail. The short version: a scholarship surplus is only a windfall if you confirm it exists, account for the taxes, and keep it from counting against you next year.
