College admissions guidance is quietly one of the most unevenly distributed resources in American education. The American School Counselor Association recommends one counselor for every 250 students, and the national average has run well above that for years. At a well-funded private school, a dedicated college counselor might sit down with each senior several times, build a balanced list, and flag deadlines. At an under-resourced public school, one counselor may be juggling scheduling, mental health, and discipline for hundreds of students, and college planning gets whatever time is left. Often that's close to none.

The students who lose the most in that setup are the ones without a parent or older sibling who has been through the process. They don't know what "reach" means in practice, which schools they're actually competitive for, or that their state flagship might be a far better bet than they assume. That's the gap [CollegeIntel](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/) is well positioned to fill: a free virtual counselor that a school can point every student toward.

## What it actually does

CollegeIntel is a set of free tools, with no account and no sign-up, covering the questions a counselor usually answers in a first meeting:

- **[Chances Calculator](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/calculator):** enter an SAT or ACT score and get a Reach / Target / Likely read on any US college. This is the core of building a balanced list, and it's exactly the conversation many students never get to have with an adult.
- **[Good-Fit Finder](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/good-fit):** instead of starting from a school, start from the student. Enter a score plus preferences for public or private, setting, size, and region, and it surfaces schools where the student lands in the typical admitted range. That's the step that turns "I've only heard of five colleges" into a real list.
- **[Major-Fit Quiz](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/major-fit):** 30 questions based on the Department of Labor's O*NET Interest Profiler, returning three suggested majors along with real earnings data and schools strong in those fields. For a student who doesn't know what they want to study, this is a structured starting point instead of a shrug.
- **[National Merit Calculator](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/national-merit-calculator):** for PSAT takers who might be in range of National Merit recognition and the scholarships that come with it, which is money a student can easily leave on the table by not knowing to look.
- **College browsing and articles:** school profiles, a near-me search, rankings, and explainers on admissions strategy, testing, financial aid, and college life. There's also a UK and Ireland section for students considering applying abroad.

## Why it works for schools with limited resources

A few design choices make it especially well suited to under-resourced settings.

**It's free with no login.** No accounts means no student data to manage, no district approval process for a new platform, and no barrier for a student using a library computer or a phone. A teacher can put the link on a slide and every student can use it that day.

**The data is honest.** Every number (admit rates, test score ranges, cost, graduation rates, earnings by major) comes from the U.S. Department of Education's College Scorecard. When a school doesn't report a figure, the site says so rather than inventing a plausible-looking number. It also doesn't pretend to calculate a precise admission percentage from GPA, because no public dataset supports that. For a student without an adult double-checking what they read, a tool that refuses to overstate its certainty is worth a lot.

**It scales the part of counseling that's repetitive.** "Where am I competitive?" and "what schools should I look at?" are questions a counselor answers over and over. Letting a tool handle that first pass frees the human counselor for the parts only a person can do: reading an essay draft, talking through family finances, writing a recommendation.

## How a school could use it

- **Junior-year classroom session:** one class period where every student runs the Good-Fit Finder and Major-Fit Quiz and leaves with a draft list of 8–10 schools across reach, target, and likely.
- **Counselor prep:** ask students to bring their CollegeIntel results to their first counseling meeting, so the limited time is spent refining a list rather than starting from zero.
- **Family nights:** walk parents through the Chances Calculator and the cost and earnings data, especially families going through the process for the first time.
- **Advisory or homeroom:** assign the articles on financial aid and testing as short readings across the fall of senior year.

## What it doesn't replace

It's worth being clear about the limits. CollegeIntel won't read a personal essay, fill out the FAFSA with a family, or write a recommendation letter, and its admission reads are based on test scores rather than a student's full application. It's a virtual counselor for the research and list-building stage, not a substitute for a caring adult. But for a school where the realistic alternative is no guidance at all, getting every student to a well-informed, balanced college list is a huge step, and it costs the school nothing.

If you work at a school like that, or you're a student whose counselor just doesn't have the time, [CollegeIntel](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/) is worth bookmarking.
